Abstract

Question. Which methods exist for estimating the state of dynamic systems that combine Markov switching with discontinuous (step, impulsive) disturbances, where the discontinuities are modelled with generalized functions (Schwartz distributions such as the Dirac delta and the Heaviside step), and how are they used in aircraft navigation?

Results. The central finding is negative: no paper was found that combines Markov-switching state estimation with an explicit generalized-function (distribution) model of the disturbances for aircraft navigation, in English, Russian or Ukrainian sources. The two ingredients exist separately. Markov-jump estimation (GPB, IMM, particle and variational filters) is well documented [1]–[5],[10],[11]; rigorous distributional treatment of impulses is documented mainly for descriptor (singular) systems [17]; and jump or impulsive Kalman-type filters exist without an explicit distribution calculus in their abstracts [18],[19],[21],[23]. In navigation, multiple-model evidence for aircraft is mostly simulation-based and self-reported [26],[29],[33]–[35]; certified aviation integrity relies on RAIM and solution-separation ARAIM rather than IMM [36]–[38]. A small illustrative simulation by the research team (1-D, two modes) shows that an IMM absorbs a velocity impulse into its manoeuvre mode but cannot see a position-measurement bias step, and that a heuristic GLR jump estimator partly repairs the bias case but fails on the impulse. An own analytical section maps delta and Heaviside disturbance models onto impulsive-input and bias-step estimation.

Limitations. The literature search used a general web search engine and page fetches only; Crossref, IEEE Xplore (including TAES), IEEE TAC full texts and several repositories were not reachable or not searched; many items were read at abstract level only. The absence of a combined paper is a statement about this search, not proof that none exists. The simulation is model-matched, 1-D, single-seed, and is not literature evidence.

Key conclusions

  1. Synthesis The combination "Markov switching + generalized-function disturbance model + aircraft navigation estimation" was not found in this search. This is a search result, not a proof of non-existence (claim C1, evidence matrix).
  2. Established fact/Study result IMM is a moment-matching approximation, not an optimal filter, even for linear Gaussian jump systems; it costs about r Kalman filters versus r2 for GPB2 and exponential growth for the exact filter [5],[11],[4].
  3. Study result Rigorous distribution-based (delta and derivatives) theory exists for descriptor systems, including rank criteria for impulse observability [17]; that is the nearest verified foundation, and it is not a Markov-switching estimation result.
  4. Synthesis For aircraft navigation, no independently verified flight-test validation of IMM was found; the quantitative IMM results located are author-reported simulations [26],[29]. One IMM-style INS/GNSS paper claims "simulation and experimental results" without numbers on the page read [27], and another's data provenance is unresolved [28].
  5. Synthesis Certified aviation integrity monitoring is built on RAIM/FDE and ARAIM solution separation, which give deterministic protection levels; no standards text endorsing IMM for integrity was found [36]–[40].
  6. Own analytical conclusion A delta in the process input corresponds to a state jump and a Heaviside in the measurement to a bias step; the two need different remedies. A Markov mode bank without a bias state cannot estimate a measurement bias (analytically unobservable with position-only data when the bias is unmodelled), and the team's simulation agrees (Section 6).
  7. Study result (illustrative simulation, not literature) In the team's 1-D simulation IMM reduced steady-state position RMSE to 2.84 m (maneuver-tuned KF 3.21 m), did not help on a +30 m bias step (late position RMSE 30.10 m versus 30.14 m), and was not better than the maneuver-tuned KF in the first 20 s after a +5 m/s velocity impulse.

1. Introduction: definitions and scope

A jump Markov (Markov-switching) system is a system whose parameters evolve according to a finite-state Markov chain. Doucet and Andrieu define jump Markov linear systems (JMLS) as "linear systems whose parameters evolve with time according to a finite state Markov chain" [4]. The Russian-language school uses the term systems of random structure [14],[15]. A discontinuous disturbance in this report means a step (a persistent bias or level shift), an impulse (a short pulse approximated by a Dirac delta), an outlier, or a mode switch. Generalized functions are Schwartz distributions, notably the Dirac delta δ(t) and the Heaviside step H(t) with H′ = δ. Aircraft navigation here covers INS/GNSS integration, manoeuvre tracking and GNSS integrity monitoring.

Scope: (a) estimation theory for Markov-switching systems; (b) use of generalized functions to model discontinuities in estimation; (c) the navigation applications and the evidence of their validation; (d) a small simulation that probes the gap; (e) an analytic discussion of how δ and H models map to practical estimation problems. Out of scope: control design, non-aviation applications except where they are the only evidence, and any claim about proprietary avionics.

3. Theoretical foundations

Equations marked textbook form, not re-verified against a fetched source come from general knowledge of the report writer. Equations marked "as in [n]" are transcribed from the notes' record of that source. Equations marked "own" are written by the team for this report.

3.1 Markov jump systems and the Bayes recursion

Established fact A JMLS has a continuous state xk and a discrete mode rk on r values with transition matrix Π = [πij]. A common discrete-time form is (textbook form, not re-verified against a fetched source):

\[ x_k = F_{r_k} x_{k-1} + G_{r_k} w_k,\qquad z_k = H_{r_k} x_k + v_k,\qquad P(r_k=j\mid r_{k-1}=i)=\pi_{ij}. \]

Study result The exact Bayes filter conditions on the whole mode history, so the number of Gaussian hypotheses grows as rn after n steps; Doucet and Andrieu state that exact MMSE/MAP computation "involves a prohibitive computational cost exponential in the number ... of observations" [4], and the same exponential growth is stated for the optimal approach in [13] (only part of that note was visible).

3.2 GPB1, GPB2 and IMM

Study result According to Pulford's survey (full text summarised) GPB(N) collapses the mode-conditioned mixture to a single Gaussian per step and needs rN Kalman filters; GPB1 runs r filters, GPB2 runs r2 filters and "quickly becomes unworkable"; IMM runs r filters, each started from a mixed initial condition, which reduces the cost "almost to that of the GPB(1) approach while obtaining performance near that of the GPB(2) approach", with no explicit manoeuvre detector [5]. The two probability equations below are as in [5]; with πij = pij and mode probabilities μi:

\[ \mu_{i|j}(k-1|k-1)=\frac{p_{ij}\,\mu_i(k-1)}{c_j},\quad c_j=\sum_i p_{ij}\mu_i(k-1) \qquad\text{(as in [5])} \]

\[ \mu_j(k)=\frac{\Lambda_j(k)\sum_i p_{ij}\mu_i(k-1)}{c},\quad \Lambda_j(k)=\mathcal N\{y(k);\hat y^{\,j}(k|k-1),S^{j}(k)\} \qquad\text{(as in [5])} \]

The mixed initial condition and the output combination are (textbook form, not re-verified against a fetched source; also implemented in the team's simulation, Section 6):

\[ x_{0j}=\sum_i \mu_{i|j}\,\hat x_i,\quad P_{0j}=\sum_i \mu_{i|j}\big[P_i+(\hat x_i-x_{0j})(\hat x_i-x_{0j})^{\top}\big],\quad \hat x=\sum_j \mu_j \hat x_j. \]

Study result "Even for the linear system case, the IMM algorithm is not optimal", with complexity about O(M s3) for M filters of state dimension s [11] (a preprint proposing a competing method). The standard textbook is [42] (not read). The original IMM note is Blom and Bar-Shalom 1988 [1] (a four-page IEEE TAC note; not read); the widely used tutorial is [2]. The second-order variant is [6]. A variational approach for switching state-space models is [7] (page-level only). A 2023 survey covers hidden Markov jump systems (asynchronous mode observation), abstract level only [9]. Rao-Blackwellised particle filters [3] and variational mixture approximations [10] are the more recent alternatives; the notes record only titles and abstracts for these, so the mechanism of [3] is unverified here.

Study result Documented limitations: transition-matrix sensitivity (one study finds IMM insensitive if the matrix is diagonally dominant, another finds strong dependence) [5]; careful model-set selection [5]; sensitivity in IMM-MHT [12]; dependence on known noise covariances [10],[11]. Author interpretation Mode-probability lag follows from a Markov prior with small switching probability; no fetched source states it explicitly.

3.3 Generalized functions, impulsive and singular systems

Established fact Distribution identities used below (textbook form, not re-verified against a fetched source): H′(t) = δ(t); ∫ f(t) δ(t−τ) dt = f(τ); the convolution of a smooth system response with δ gives the impulse response.

Study result For descriptor systems E x′ = A x, y = C x, x(0) = w, classical solutions generally do not exist; the response is distributional and may contain δ(t) and its derivatives. An impulse of order p is λ(t) = δ(t)v0 + … + δ(p)(t)vp, vp ≠ 0. The impulse-observability criterion (case r = 0), as recorded from [17]:

\[ \operatorname{rank}\begin{bmatrix} E & A\\ 0 & E\\ 0 & C\end{bmatrix} = n + \operatorname{rank}(E)\qquad\text{(as in [17])} \]

The source was read through a tool summary; the journal and year of its final version were not verified [17]. Author interpretation This is the rigorous distributional apparatus found in an estimation/observer context; applying it to Markov-jump navigation filters would be an extension, not an established result found here.

Author interpretation (flagged as unverified textbook knowledge in the notes) Products of distributions such as δ·H or δ2 are undefined in Schwartz theory, which limits multiplicative or state-dependent jumps; Colombeau-type algebras or Filippov-type regularisations are usual remedies; Itô and Stratonovich readings differ for state-dependent jump amplitudes. No source for these statements was retrieved, so they should be treated as a pointer for verification.

4. Main analysis

4.1 The central finding: no combined literature found

Synthesis Searches in English, Russian and Ukrainian, using phrases such as "generalized functions Markov switching estimation navigation", "узагальнені функції марковські перемикання оцінювання навігація" and "обобщённые функции фильтр Калмана разрывные возмущения", found no paper combining Markov switching with generalized-function (δ/H) models for estimation in navigation or aviation [notes: generalized-functions search log]. Ukrainian National Aviation University navigation papers that surfaced use Kalman filter banks and chi-square tests [24] or a general Kalman justification [25], with neither generalized functions nor Markov switching. The brief's premise that such a literature exists was therefore not confirmed.

Author interpretation This is absence in a limited search: a general-purpose engine, abstract-level reads, no Vernadsky/eLibrary/OpenAlex/Semantic Scholar queries, and unopened leads from Ukrainian and Russian repositories remain (listed in the notes). The result should be read as "not found", with some probability that the Russian-language random-structure literature (Sinitsyn, Kazakov, Bukhalev) uses distributional language in full texts that abstracts do not show. The notes also report that the "Kuznetsov" named in the brief could not be identified with generalized-function estimation (the Kuznetsov found is a probabilist).

4.3 What the navigation literature shows

Evidence for multiple-model methods. Study result Oshman and Koifman (JGCD 2006) used an IMM with a bank of EKFs, one per hypothesis of which satellite is spoofed; with a constant 100 m bias on one satellite the IMM kept 1-sigma horizontal error at or below about 10 m while a snapshot parity-space RAIM sometimes misidentified a healthy satellite and reached errors near 140 m; under periodically switching spoofing IMM stayed under 10 m versus 80 m for RAIM; two simultaneous spoofers defeated the single-spoofer bank. The data were simulation only and self-reported [26]. Author interpretation The RAIM baseline is a snapshot method, not a modern FDE/ARAIM implementation, so the advantage should not be extrapolated [notes, inference].

Study result A 2024 IEEE Access paper claims 13.5% (EKF) and 1.8% (IEKF) improvement with GNSS available and up to 93% and 96% during outages for a Markov-chain fusion of IEKF and EKF; the page read does not say whether data were real or simulated and does not call the method IMM [28]. Jwo et al. 2013 apply IMM nonlinear filters to vehicle GPS/INS and mention "simulation and experimental results" without numbers on the page [27]. A 2023 preprint on IMM-KF for ADS-B trajectories used simulated data; its claimed 58.98% and 28.56% RMSE reductions are against raw ADS-B and an oracle-tuned baseline, and a plain IMM-KF with empirical noise settings diverged near a 400 s turn [29]. Real aircraft data with a single-model EKF exist (aerobatic flights, [30]) but are not evidence for IMM; real-data UAV work with adaptive single filters [31] and a simulation-only belief-adaptive EKF [32] likewise are not IMM evidence. Synthesis No independently verified flight-test validation of IMM in aircraft navigation was found.

Discontinuity types in practice. Study result Tanil et al. state, citing prior work, that the worst-case fault profile "is neither a step, a ramp, nor a quadratic function", and derive worst-case slopes from a single filter's innovations (simulation/covariance analysis only) [33]; a smart spoofer can start with a small growing fault so INS calibration drifts without tripping the innovation monitor (simulated Boeing 747 approach, integrity risk about 5.9e-6 for a worst-case 140 s attack) [34]. Quartararo and Langel's bank of finite-length cumulative innovation monitors detects slowly accumulating faults in simulation (99% detection for a 500 m fault within 130 s at Pfa 1e-5) and is explicitly not multiple-model [35]; a 4% covariance scaling error shifts false-alarm probability by two to three orders of magnitude [35]. Author interpretation Step-only benchmarks understate the integrity problem.

Standards and integrity. Study result ARAIM is based on solution separation and aims at LPV-200 with dual-constellation GPS plus Galileo; stated assumptions include a satellite fault rate of 1e-5 per hour per satellite and 1.3e-8 per approach for multiple faults (assumptions of the GEAS proposal, via a secondary ESA wiki summary) [36]. Blanch et al. describe all-in-view plus fault-tolerant subset solutions with separation thresholds setting protection levels; their example uses 20 single and 190 pair subsets for 20 satellites and an illustrative VPL of 19.7 m [37]. Established fact The RTCA SC-159 Terms of Reference (Rev 26, approved 2026-06-23) schedule DO-401A/ED-259B with H-ARAIM, define ABAS as RAIM plus AAIM, and list a new deliverable for dual-frequency multi-constellation GNSS-aided inertial systems [38]; ED-259A is the current MOPS for dual-frequency multi-constellation SBAS airborne equipment (secondary listing) [39]. An EASA consultation confirms the existence of RAIM/FDE requirements for ETSO-C145 but is old [40]. Synthesis No standards text endorsing IMM or multiple-model adaptive estimation for aviation integrity was found; ICAO Annex 10 and current TSO text were not fetched.

Author interpretation (author reasoning, not a source claim) IMM supplies a probabilistic state estimate but no deterministic protection-level bound, which is the likely obstacle to certification credit. Because mode probabilities depend on tuned transition probabilities and noise levels, a separate integrity-risk analysis would be needed.

4.4 Own analysis: delta and Heaviside models versus impulsive-input and bias-step estimation

Labelled as analytical reasoning by the report team (Own analytical conclusion). It rests on standard linear-system facts (textbook form, not re-verified against a fetched source) and on stated assumptions; it is not a result from the literature and it was not checked with a formal proof. Assumptions: linear time-invariant dynamics, known (F, G, H), Gaussian white noise, a small number of isolated discontinuities, and one jump at most per analysis window. Limits are given at the end of the subsection.

(i) Impulsive input. Take the continuous-time model \(\dot x = A x + B\,(w(t)+u(t))\) with \(u(t)=d\,\delta(t-\tau)\). By the sifting property the solution is \(x(t)=\Phi(t,t_0)x_0+\int \Phi(t,s)B(w+u)\,ds\), so the delta contributes \(\Phi(t,\tau)\,B\,d\,H(t-\tau)\): the state undergoes a jump \(\Delta x = B d\) at \(\tau\) and then evolves freely. For a double integrator with acceleration input, \(B=[0,1]^{\top}\): an acceleration delta of weight d is a velocity step of size d (a Heaviside in v) and a position ramp \(d\,(t-\tau)H(t-\tau)\).

(ii) Bias step. A measurement model \(z=Hx+b\,H(t-\tau)+v\) has a Heaviside in the measurement channel. With an unmodelled b, the filter's innovation acquires a persistent mean; in a Kalman filter that is stable and consistent with the model, the state estimate absorbs it. With an augmented constant bias state \(b\) (\(\dot b=0\)), the augmented observation row for position-only data is \([1,0,1]\) acting on \((p,v,b)\): position and bias enter identically, so the pair is not observable from position alone; observability requires other information (a different sensor type, or dynamics that tie position to another measurement). The Heaviside step is the integral of a delta, so a "bias step" is, in innovation space, an impulse that has been integrated by the measurement path; this is why impulse and bias hypotheses have different signatures in a GLR test.

(iii) Mapping to detection. The two hypotheses are linear in the unknown magnitude m: innovation \(\nu_k = \nu^{0}_k + m\,s_k\), where the signature \(s_k\) is the filter's response to a unit \(\delta\) (impulse) or unit step (bias). The GLR statistic \(\ell=a^{2}/b\), with \(a=\sum s_k\nu_k/S_k\) and \(b=\sum s_k^{2}/S_k\), is the same form used in the team's simulation (Section 6) and follows the GLR jump-detection idea of Willsky and Jones [44] (not retrieved). The delta/H representation therefore gives a systematic way to generate signatures, which is the practical use of the formalism in a discrete-time filter.

(iv) Joining with Markov switching. A jump process with known amplitude can be encoded as an extra mode whose input is \(d\,\delta\); with a Gaussian prior on d, the mode becomes a high-variance process-noise mode with mean zero, which is exactly what a "manoeuvre" mode of an IMM already represents. Own analytical conclusion This explains the simulation outcome that the IMM's manoeuvre mode absorbs most of a velocity impulse without a dedicated detector. A bias step in the measurement channel is not representable by a process-noise-level mode set at all: it needs a mode whose measurement equation has an offset, i.e. a bias-augmented model with sufficient observability.

(v) Discrete-time remark. In sampled filters every disturbance is a finite sum; distributions are not needed for rigour, only for exposition and for deriving the signature. In the team's simulation \(\delta\) is represented as \(u_k=d/\Delta t\) over one step, giving \(\Delta v = d\) and \(\Delta p = \tfrac12 d\,\Delta t\) (for d = 5 m/s and \(\Delta t=1\) s: +5 m/s and +2.5 m).

Limits of this reasoning. Multiplicative or state-dependent jumps involve products of distributions, which Schwartz theory does not define (textbook, unverified; Section 3.3); a continuous-time stochastic treatment with random jump times needs jump-diffusion or random-measure tools rather than a bare δ; the IMM's innovations are not white, so the GLR statistic is approximate; and none of this was verified against a published derivation. Hypothesis A combined estimator (mode bank for process-noise levels, plus bias-augmented or δ/H-signature hypotheses with Markov-governed switching of the discontinuity type) might outperform either component; no evidence for this was gathered beyond the single illustrative simulation.

6. Formulas and calculations: illustrative simulation

This section is an illustrative simulation by the research team (a subagent of the same model). It is not literature evidence. Every number below is copied from the team's simulation note [43] and its results.json; no other numbers were computed by the report writer. It was produced with a fixed code file (about 4 s runtime), common random numbers across scenarios, and no re-runs after seeing results.

6.1 Setup

State x = [p, v] (along-track position in m and velocity in m/s), time step 1 s, 301 epochs (300 s). Truth: \(x_k=F x_{k-1}+G(a_k+u_k)\), with \(F=\begin{bmatrix}1&\Delta t\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\), \(G=[\Delta t^2/2,\ \Delta t]^{\top}\), piecewise-constant acceleration \(a_k\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_{r_k}^2)\) with \(\sigma=0.05\) m/s2 (mode 1, cruise) or 1.0 m/s2 (mode 2, maneuver). The Markov chain has \(\Pi=\begin{bmatrix}0.98&0.02\\0.05&0.95\end{bmatrix}\); the realised fraction of time in mode 2 was 0.264 (stationary value 2/7 = 0.286). Measurement: \(z_k=p_k+w_k\), \(w_k\sim\mathcal N(0,5^2)\) m every second. Prior covariance \(P_0=\mathrm{diag}(25\ \mathrm{m^2},\,1\ (\mathrm{m/s})^2)\); IMM initial mode probabilities [0.9, 0.1] (the team's choice).

Scenarios: none; bias: +30 m added to \(z_k\) from k = 150 s; impulse: \(u_{100}=5/\Delta t=5\) m/s2 for one step, so v jumps by +5 m/s and p by +2.5 m at t = 100 s (a consequence of the piecewise-constant model). Monte Carlo: N = 500 runs, one generator seed (20261029). Filters: (1) KF with q = 0.05 m/s2 ("cruise-tuned"); (2) KF with q = 1.0 m/s2 ("maneuver-tuned"); (3) two-mode IMM with the true \(\Pi\) and both q values; (4) IMM plus a windowed GLR jump estimator.

6.2 Equations used by the simulation

Kalman filter per mode j with \(Q_j=\sigma_j^2 GG^{\top}\) (Joseph-form covariance update) and the IMM steps (mixing, mode-matched filtering, likelihood, output) were implemented as in the textbook forms of Section 3.2 (textbook form, not re-verified against a fetched source). NEES is \(\varepsilon_k=(\hat x-x)^{\top}P^{-1}(\hat x-x)\) with 2 degrees of freedom, so the ideal mean is 2.

GLR jump estimator (a heuristic, described in the note): two hypothesis families started at every epoch within the last W = 20 epochs: (B) a bias step of unknown size in the measurement from epoch \(\theta\); (I) a velocity impulse entering at \(\theta\) (acceleration delta, error vector \(e=[\Delta t/2,\ 1]\,\Delta v\)). With unit-size signature \(s_k\) and combined IMM innovation \(\bar\nu_k\), covariance \(\bar S_k\):

\[ a=\sum_k \frac{s_k\bar\nu_k}{\bar S_k},\qquad b=\sum_k\frac{s_k^{2}}{\bar S_k},\qquad \hat m=\frac{a}{b},\qquad \ell=\frac{a^{2}}{b}. \]

If the maximum \(\ell\) exceeds h = 25.0 (chi-square, 1 dof; p about 6e-7 per test), the state is corrected by \(\hat m\) times the signature, and for type B the accumulated bias estimate is subtracted from later measurements; hypotheses are then cleared. It assumes white Gaussian innovations (approximate for an IMM) and uses an effective gain \(K_{\mathrm{eff}}=\sum_j\mu_jK_j\). W and h were fixed before running and not tuned.

6.5 Interpretation (Study result within the simulation; Own analytical conclusion beyond it)

  1. Steady state with manoeuvres: IMM has the lowest RMSE and ANEES near 2 (1.98); the maneuver-tuned KF is conservative (ANEES about 1.3) and about 13% worse in position RMSE (overall 3.18 m versus 2.81 m); the cruise-tuned KF is badly inconsistent (ANEES about 100) because it cannot follow the manoeuvres, 26% of the time.
  2. Bias step: IMM does not help at all (late position RMSE 30.10 m versus 30.14 m); both absorb the +30 m as truth, and IMM is less consistent than the maneuver KF (ANEES 196.61 versus 134.33). A bias in position-only data is unobservable to a mode bank with no bias state.
  3. Impulse: IMM is not better than the maneuver-tuned KF in the transient (3.67 m versus 4.01 m position RMSE, ANEES 2.54 versus 6.56, recovery 6.1 s versus 8.5 s). A diagnostic run in the note found a mean mode-2 probability of 0.28 at t = 100 s, 0.60 at t = 103 s and 0.82 at t = 105 s. Author interpretation This is consistent with Markov-prior lag, the mechanism flagged as inference in Section 3.2.
  4. GLR on the bias step: helps partially (late position RMSE 16.00 m versus 30.10 m; position recovery 64.4% of runs versus 0%); it does not reach the baseline; detection happens in 86.6% of runs within 30 s and some are classified as impulses. The team did not separately verify what dominates the residual 16 m.
  5. GLR on the impulse: fails and slightly hurts. It triggered in 4 of 500 runs; in one run it later declared a roughly 26 m bias (false classification) and compensated measurements, raising the late position RMSE from 2.78 m to 3.03 m (CI [2.53, 3.45] versus [2.74, 2.83] for IMM). The correction step has no safety net.

6.6 Limitations of the simulation (as stated in the note)

  • Simulation only, with filter models matching the truth generator except for the disturbances (a favourable case for IMM); no real flight data, no model mismatch in noise levels or transition matrix.
  • 1-D, two modes, linear-Gaussian, position-only measurements; no INS/GNSS/clock states, no integrity monitoring.
  • The GLR estimator is a heuristic add-on: effective gain for the IMM, white-innovation assumption, one hand-set threshold (h = 25, W = 20), no sensitivity study; other settings could change the impulse-detection result.
  • Recovery times depend on the definition (5-epoch moving RMS, 1.5 times own steady-state value, 100 s censoring) and are not comparable across filters with different baselines. Only one seed set was run; CIs reflect the 500 runs, not seed variation. NEES CIs are normal approximations.
  • The impulse scenario also causes a +2.5 m position jump because of the discretisation.
  • Own analytical conclusion Consequently IMM does not help on the bias step and the detector fails on the impulse in this configuration; neither result should be generalised to real navigation filters.

7. Critical analysis

Strength of the central negative. Author interpretation The "no combined paper" result is only as strong as the search. The most plausible hiding place is Russian-language random-structure filtering (Sinitsyn, Kazakov, Bukhalev) where full texts may use distributional notation that abstracts omit, and Ukrainian aviation-university repositories that were not systematically searched. A second plausible explanation for the absence is that the concept is not needed: in sampled filters impulses and steps are finite sums, and the practical content is carried by GLR or augmented-state methods (Section 4.4).

Quality of the navigation evidence. Synthesis Almost all numeric performance claims located are author-reported simulation (Section 4.3). The best-sourced numeric item, Oshman and Koifman, is a single group's simulation against a snapshot baseline [26]; the 2023 ADS-B preprint uses a baseline tuned with future information [29]; the 2024 outage figures come from an abstract that does not state the data type [28]. Patent assertions surfaced (three US patents, assignees and claims not examined) and are not evidence [notes, navigation section 5].

Steelman for IMM. IMM remains a sound answer to unknown manoeuvre modes: the team's simulation shows a clear steady-state gain over single filters, and a model-matched setting is where a Markov-switching bank should do best. The criticism here is narrower: a process-noise mode set does not address measurement bias steps, and certification relevance is undemonstrated.

Steelman for generalized functions. The formalism is the natural language for continuous-time impulses and for singular systems, and it is the clean way to derive step and impulse signatures. The counter-position is that, for digital filters, it adds exposition more than capability.

Methodological weaknesses. Shared model and tools across notes; tool-summarised reads; unverified DOIs; Mazor et al.'s year conflict (page says 1988, usual citation is 1998); the Blom and Bar-Shalom entry is metadata only; the textbook equations in Sections 3 and 4.4 were not re-checked against a fetched primary source.

8. Practical application

Own analytical conclusion The following are design implications drawn from the notes and the team's reasoning, not validated guidance.

  • Use a manoeuvre-mode bank (IMM or a tuned single filter) for process-side switching such as velocity or acceleration changes; the simulation shows a gain of this kind in steady state, and the manoeuvre mode absorbs impulse-like inputs, albeit with a few seconds of lag.
  • Do not expect a process-noise mode bank to detect measurement bias steps. Add either an explicit bias state with a second measurement source for observability, or a hypothesis test with step signatures, or a solution-separation style monitor.
  • Do not rely on IMM for integrity claims. Certified practice is RAIM/FDE and solution-separation ARAIM with protection levels [36]–[38]; fault profiles of concern are not limited to steps [33],[34].
  • If an add-on jump corrector is used, it needs a safety net: in the simulation one false classification persisted and increased late error.
  • For certification-oriented work, track the SC-159 deliverables for DFMC GNSS-aided inertial systems [38] and plan an independent integrity-risk analysis for any multiple-model filter (Author interpretation).

9. Open questions

  1. Does any Russian- or Ukrainian-language full text on random-structure systems (Sinitsyn, Kazakov, Bukhalev, Kharkiv/Kyiv aviation schools) use generalized functions for jump disturbances in navigation filtering? (Not determinable from abstracts.)
  2. Is there independent flight-test evidence for IMM or MMAE in INS/GNSS (IEEE Xplore, TAES, NAVIGATION, GPS Solutions not searched)? What are the data and baseline in [28]?
  3. Can multiple-model estimators be given deterministic or overbounded integrity guarantees comparable to solution separation?
  4. How do results change under model mismatch, with multi-seed repetition, in 3-D with INS/GNSS states, and with tuned GLR thresholds? (Hypothesis that a combined mode-plus-signature estimator is competitive; untested.)
  5. What are the verified stability and error-bound theorems for IMM and for MJLS filters (the Costa-Fragoso-Marques theory, H-infinity filters)? None was verified here.
  6. Which regularisation (Colombeau, Filippov, jump-diffusion) is appropriate for state-dependent jump amplitudes, and what is its role in estimation?

10. Final conclusions

Synthesis On the evidence reachable in this session, estimation for Markov-switching systems is a mature but approximate field (GPB, IMM, particle and variational filters) [1]–[5],[10]; rigorous generalized-function treatment of impulses is established mainly for descriptor systems [17]; aircraft navigation uses multiple-model ideas chiefly in simulation-based research [26],[29], while certified integrity relies on RAIM and solution separation [36]–[38]. No work combining the three was found; this is a statement about the search, not about the literature.

Own analytical conclusion The combination is conceptually coherent: a delta in the input is a state jump that a manoeuvre mode can absorb, whereas a Heaviside in the measurement is a bias step that a process-noise mode bank cannot see without bias augmentation. The team's illustrative simulation agrees in direction (IMM no help on the bias step, GLR partial help, GLR failure on the impulse), but it is a single-seed, model-matched, 1-D experiment and carries no weight as literature evidence. The most valuable next steps are a systematic search of Russian- and Ukrainian-language full texts and of IEEE and ION databases, and a multi-seed, mismatched-model, 3-D replication.

Appendix A. Glossary

ABAS / AAIM
Aircraft-based augmentation system / aircraft autonomous integrity monitoring (RAIM plus other onboard sensors) [38].
ANEES / NEES
(Average) normalised estimation error squared; ideal mean equals the state dimension (2 here).
ARAIM
Advanced RAIM, based on multiple-hypothesis solution separation [36],[37].
Descriptor (singular) system
E x′ = A x with singular E; solutions may contain δ and derivatives [17].
Dirac delta δ(t)
Generalized function with unit integral concentrated at zero; derivative of the Heaviside step.
GLR
Generalized likelihood ratio test for unknown-magnitude jumps (Willsky-Jones style, [44]).
GPB1, GPB2
Generalized pseudo-Bayesian filters of order 1 and 2 (r and r2 filters) [5].
Heaviside step H(t)
Zero for t < 0, one for t > 0.
IMM
Interacting multiple model filter [1],[2],[5].
JMLS / MJLS
(Markov) jump linear system [4].
RAIM / FDE
Receiver autonomous integrity monitoring / fault detection and exclusion.
Random-structure system
Russian-school term for a system whose structure switches randomly [14],[15].
Schwartz distribution
Generalized function defined as a continuous linear functional on test functions.
Solution separation
Comparing a full solution with fault-tolerant subset solutions to bound position error [37].
